# Cryptocurrency markets

The microstructure of Chapter 3 assumes one consolidated book, a trading day with an open and a close, and a small constant fee. None of that survives the move to crypto. The same asset trades on dozens of unaffiliated centralised exchanges (CEXs) and on-chain pools at once, the session never ends, a trade costs a proportional venue fee plus a fixed per-transaction gas charge, and the venue carrying most of the volume is a perpetual future whose price is pinned to spot by a cash flow called the funding rate. This chapter sets out what those differences do to execution, and separates what the literature measures from what remains a judgement.

## The institutional differences that matter

**Continuous trading, no circuit breakers.** Crypto CEXs run 24 hours a day with no auction and no exchange-level halt, so there is no closing price to benchmark a VWAP schedule against and no consolidated open to anchor an arrival price; the arrival price must be defined against a chosen reference venue rather than a tape.

**Venue fragmentation without a consolidated tape.** The same asset quotes on many exchanges that share no book, with spreads, depth and fee tiers differing by orders of magnitude, so routing (Chapter 9) is the central problem rather than a detail.

**Tiered maker–taker fees.** CEX fees are fractions of traded notional tiered by realised 30-day volume. Binance's spot schedule at the time of writing runs from 0.1000% maker and 0.1000% taker for a regular user (30-day volume below USD 1,000,000) to 0.0110% maker and 0.0230% taker at VIP 9 (at least USD 4,000,000,000), with a further 25% discount when the fee is paid in the exchange's own token; maker fees are below taker at every tier and can be negotiated to zero for market makers. Because the tier depends on volume already traded, the marginal fee is history-dependent: the same notional costs about ten times more to take than to post at the retail end.

**Funding rates.** A perpetual future has no expiry, so the exchange pays a periodic cash flow between longs and shorts to hold the contract near spot; on Binance the interval is eight hours and the rate is a function of the futures–spot premium.

**Withdrawal fees and settlement latency.** Moving an asset off an exchange costs a per-asset withdrawal fee — 10 to 50 basis points in an early survey — and settles only when the blockchain confirms, so a cross-exchange arbitrageur bears price drift over the confirmation window.

**Perpetuals and AMMs dominate.** Most volume is in perpetual futures rather than spot, and a parallel decentralised venue set — constant function market makers (CFMMs) — prices trades on a deterministic curve rather than a book, with structurally different execution costs.

## Fragmentation and arbitrage

Makarov and Schoar (2020) is the reference measurement of cross-venue dislocation. Using tick data from about 80 exchanges in 2017–2018, they find daily potential arbitrage profits often above USD 75 million, with a minimum of USD 2 billion of total potential profit over December 2017 to February 2018. The gaps are large and geographic: the average US–Korea price ratio exceeded 15% and reached 40% on several days (the "Kimchi premium"), Japan–US averaged about 10%, US–Europe about 3%, and deviations between exchanges inside one country stayed under 1%. A one-factor decomposition attributes about 80% of bitcoin returns to a common component, with the idiosyncratic venue components carrying the spreads. The costs that could close the gap are small by comparison: bid–ask spreads average 1 to 10 basis points, exchange fees run 0.25% down to 0.1% tiered by volume with zero maker fees on most venues, and withdrawal fees are 10 to 50 basis points, so the authors put the round-trip cost for large players at 50 to 75 basis points — far below the dislocation. Regressing the common return component on signed volume gives a coefficient of $8.8\times10^{-6}$ with $t=80$ and $R^2=54\%$, which they translate into "the price impact of nine basis points for a USD 1 million trade" at a bitcoin price of USD 10,000.

Hautsch, Scheuch and Voigt (2018/2023) supply the mechanism: settlement latency. A blockchain replaces the central counterparty with a consensus protocol, so a cross-exchange transfer takes blocks' worth of time during which the arbitrageur bears price risk. Their model gives a no-trade band that widens with latency and volatility, and their tests on Bitcoin network and order-book data show that large price differences coincide with high settlement latency, that asset flows chase arbitrage opportunities, and that differences across exchanges with low default risk are smaller. Holding inventory on the destination venue removes the latency but substitutes exchange default risk. Cross-venue routing is therefore a control problem over settlement time, not only a fee comparison.

Barbon and Ranaldo (2021/2024) formalise the two-layer cost stack. For a CEX the effective cost is the quoted half-spread plus the exchange fee; for a DEX it is the quoted half-spread plus the protocol fee plus gas. Because gas is fixed per transaction, its share falls as notional grows. Their sample covers 70% of CEX volume and roughly 60% of DEX volume over March 2021 to February 2023, and the conclusion is a size threshold: trading small and medium amounts is cheaper on a CEX, while DEXs become cost-effective above about USD 10,000. DEX deviations from no-arbitrage are an order of magnitude larger than CEX deviations, and using the May-2021 Uniswap v3 upgrade and gas-price variation they estimate that a one-standard-deviation increase in gas prices raises the magnitude of price deviations by 18% to 25%.

Wu, Sui, Thiery and Pai (2025) measure the value the CEX–DEX arbitrage actually extracts. From on-chain swaps they estimate that 19 major searchers extracted USD 233.8 million across 7,203,560 transactions, with the three leading searchers capturing roughly three-quarters of volume and value. Profitability is tied to block-builder integration: neutral searchers who spread flow keep higher margins, while those routing exclusively to an affiliated builder share most of the value and "sometimes even operate at negative net profit". The route — builder or searcher channel, private or public mempool — is thus itself an execution decision, priced jointly with gas and inclusion probability.

## Price impact in crypto

Donier and Bonart (2014/2015) reconstruct more than one million metaorders on the Bitcoin/USD exchange MtGox. The peak impact of a metaorder of volume $Q$, averaged over execution rates $\mu$, follows

$$\langle I(Q,\mu)\rangle_\mu \approx Y_e\, Q^{\delta},$$

with $\delta\approx0.5$ and $Y_e\approx4.5\times10^{-2}$, fitting the smallest scales over four decades. Normalising by average volatility and daily volume gives a Y-ratio of about 0.9, close to the value on mature equity and futures markets. The law also holds along the metaorder trajectory, not only at the final price, and the uninformed component of flow produces an almost complete long-term decay of impact. MtGox charged 0.6% per transaction, a spread of about 60 basis points that often exceeded the peak impact of a typical metaorder, so on that venue costs were fee-dominated — the reverse of the equity intuition.

The crypto law is the *same* square-root law as Chapter 3, not a variant. The exponent is $\delta\approx1/2$ and the normalised coefficient $Y\approx0.9$ sits inside the equity-and-futures range, even though the venue had almost no high-frequency trading or statistical arbitrage. This is out-of-sample support for the universality of the square-root law; what differs is the cost stack around it, where the fixed fee and wide spread dominate small trades. Cho, Norman and Nunes (2023) fit the transient side with a propagator, $M(t)=\int_0^t f(\dot x_s)\,G(t-s)\,ds+\int\sigma\,dZ_s$, whose decay kernel shows a large initial impact followed by reversion decaying as a power law.

Barone and Lillo (2026) test the transparency trade-off on Hyperliquid, a fully on-chain limit-order book for perpetuals with protocol-native TWAP orders visible from inception. From address-level data they reconstruct about 4.3 million hidden metaorders and about 465,000 visible TWAP executions. Hidden metaorders follow front-loaded, U-shaped schedules consistent with transient-impact optimal execution, while native TWAPs trade nearly uniformly. At median volatility, visible TWAPs have about 9 basis points lower temporary impact and leave roughly 5 basis points less post-execution price displacement; hidden metaorders trading alongside already-visible same-direction TWAP flow face higher displacement, about 0.8–0.9 basis points for a 10 percentage-point increase in same-side visible dominance. Preannouncing a large order lowers its own cost and shifts impact onto non-announcers.

## Perpetual futures and funding

The perpetual future holds most crypto liquidity, and its funding mechanism changes a position's holding cost and therefore the optimal horizon. He, Manela, Ross and von Wachter (2022/2026) give the anchoring rule used by Binance:

$$\Phi\!\left(\frac{F-S}{F}\right) = \kappa\,\frac{F-S}{F} + \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\iota - \kappa\,\frac{F-S}{F},\; -\gamma,\; \gamma\right), \qquad \operatorname{clamp}(x,l,h)=\min(\max(x,l),h),$$

with current Binance parameters $\kappa=1$, $\gamma=0.05\%$ and $\iota=0.01\%$. When futures and spot are close the rate is pinned near the small interest component $\iota$; when they diverge it grows linearly with the premium. The no-arbitrage price is the "random-maturity" price, the risk-neutral expectation of spot at a random time, mirroring the perpetual's random effective maturity.

The paper is also the clearest example of an execution model that ingests the *full fee schedule*. Arbitrageurs face a round-trip cost, and the no-arbitrage band widens as it rises. Costs come from Binance's official schedule, attributed by 30-day volume: "high" (spot above USD 1 million, futures above USD 15 million, a retail individual), "medium" (spot above USD 150 million, futures above USD 1 billion, a small fund), "low" (spot above USD 2 billion, futures above USD 12.5 billion, a large fund), with zero fees negotiable for market makers; maker rather than taker fees are used because institutions post limit orders. One-way maker fees are 0.0675% spot and 0.0144% futures at the high tier and 0.0225% and 0.0018% at the low tier, giving annualised no-arbitrage bounds of $\pm179.4\%$ and $\pm53.2\%$. The random-maturity arbitrage earns a Sharpe ratio of 3.35 under retail costs and up to 11.65 for fee-exempt market makers, falling to 3.27 and 10.46 after effective bid–ask spreads; the authors estimate those indirect costs at under 0.35 basis points, well below the direct fees. Deviations are large — the abstract reports a mean absolute deviation of about 52% to 90% per year, the results section 60% to 90%, across cryptocurrencies — shrinking by almost 80% after 2022.

Ackerer, Hugonnier and Jermann (2023/2024) make the pricing rigorous. With a funding payment linear in the futures–spot gap, $f_t=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_t[x_\theta]$, where $\theta$ is exponentially distributed with mean $1/\kappa$ and $\kappa$ the anchoring intensity; as $\kappa\to\infty$ futures and spot coincide. The basis is therefore not a pure signal but a funding-mechanism artefact. Kim and Park (2025) show the design is a choice variable: using path-dependent infinite-horizon backward stochastic differential equations they derive funding rules that keep the perpetual anchored to a target, give replicating portfolios for the issuer, and characterise the difference between path-dependent and Markovian rules, so the executable basis signal depends on the venue's rule rather than a universal constant.

Donnelly, Lin and Lorig (2026) place the Almgren–Chriss liquidation problem inside a perpetual. The agent manages transaction costs, inventory risk, permanent impact and *ongoing funding payments* on the remaining position. For the identity payoff $\psi(s)=s$ the optimal speed is closed-form,

$$\nu^*(t,q,p,s) = \frac{1}{4k}\left[\left(\xi(t)+\pi(t)\right)q + \left(\xi(t)-\pi(t)\right)\frac{p-s}{b}\right],$$

with the coefficient on the scaled funding rate $z=p-s$ negative for all $t<T$: the agent trades faster to reduce the cost of paying funding on a long or to lock in receiving it on a short. For non-linear payoffs the approximations are valid when the funding parameter or the trading interval is small, the former a perturbation of the Almgren–Chriss strategy. Because funding accrues on the *remaining* position, an effective carry on holding, the optimal window for a perpetual differs from the spot window.

## Execution on AMMs and routing

A CFMM prices by a rule, not a book. Angeris, Agrawal, Evans, Chitra and Boyd (2021) show a proposed trade $\Delta$ is accepted if and only if the trading function is non-decreasing across it, $\varphi(R-\Delta)\ge\varphi(R)$ with $R$ the reserves, and that in all practical cases (Uniswap, SushiSwap, Balancer, Curve, mStable) the trading function is concave. That concavity makes multi-asset trading tractable: choosing a trade, detecting arbitrage, reading a price oracle and executing across many pools become convex optimisation problems solved exactly, with dual variables carrying a marginal-price interpretation. Angeris, Chitra, Evans and Boyd (2021/2022) separate the two cases that define practical routing: without fixed costs, routing over a CFMM network is convex; with a fixed cost per venue — gas — it becomes a mixed-integer convex program, solvable globally only by slow methods or approximately by convex-based heuristics. Gas is a fixed per-pool cost, so routing is combinatorial, the crypto analogue of the equity fixed-venue-fee problem.

Diamandis, Resnick, Chitra and Angeris (2023) make it fast enough to run in a bot by dual decomposition: relaxing the single coupling constraint turns the problem into independent per-CFMM subproblems iterated with a dual update, accommodating concentrated-liquidity aggregate CFMMs such as Uniswap v3 and multi-fee-tier routes. Their reported "over 50%" improvement is the objective value of their solver against a Mosek baseline on test instances, not a trading-cost saving.

Xi and Moallemi (2026) audit real routing on 2.98 million WETH–USDC swaps on Ethereum mainnet. Realised routes lose, on average, 2.02 basis points per trade relative to a gas-aware full-venue optimum, aggregating to USD 24.20 million across about USD 120.42 billion of volume. Relative to the optimum conditional on the pools actually used the loss is only USD 6.58 million (a mean of 0.05467 basis points), so most of the shortfall comes from not activating additional pools. Optimising on one-block-lagged state rather than execution-time state adds 1.29 basis points under a no-gas optimum (about USD 15.5 million) and 1.78 basis points under the gas-aware optimum (about USD 21.4 million), so much of the sub-optimality is a timing mismatch, not bad splitting. Losses are heavy-tailed, and sandwiched trades show a pronounced upward shift in both level and dispersion.

Cartea, Drissi and Monga (2023/2025) give the AMM analogue of Almgren–Chriss. The execution cost of a CFMM is the curvature of its trading function: for a constant-product pool with depth $\kappa$ and marginal rate $Z$, the level function has

$$\varphi''(q^Y)=\frac{2\kappa^2}{(q^Y)^3}=\frac{2Z^{3/2}}{\kappa},$$

and the convexity cost of a trade of size $y$ is approximately $\tfrac12\varphi''(q^Y_O)\,y^2$. The unit cost is therefore *linear in trade size* and *inversely proportional to depth* — unlike a limit-order book, where the per-unit cost is set by the book. Convexity costs are stochastic as LPs add and remove liquidity, and the authors solve the taker's exchange-rate-risk-versus-execution-cost problem in three settings — reference rate forming on a competing CEX, in the pool, or in both — deriving a closed-form approximation deployable in real time. Lehar, Parlour and Zoican (2023/2024) explain why a router must split across fee tiers rather than always hit the cheapest pool: facing a fixed common gas cost, high-fee pools attract 58% of liquidity supply yet execute only 21% of volume, large LPs dominate the low-fee pools, and small LPs converge to high-fee pools to mitigate adverse selection, so liquidity is endogenously parked across tiers. Their study pairs Uniswap fee tiers of 1, 5, 30 and 100 basis points with ticks of 1, 10, 60 and 200 basis points.

The essential difference from an order book is that an AMM prices against a continuous curve while a book quotes discrete levels. The AMM's cost is deterministic given reserves and known before the trade, so slippage can be computed rather than discovered; but the liquidity provider bears the mirror-image loss. Cartea, Drissi and Monga (2023/2024) decompose the LP's wealth into fee income, the mark-to-market of the pool's holdings and gas rebalancing costs, isolating predictable loss — the unhedgeable loss from the pool mechanically selling the appreciating asset — which increases as a concentrated-liquidity range narrows. This is the concentrated-liquidity analogue of loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR) and the floor on AMM execution cost: a taker cannot trade cheaper than the fee plus the predictable loss the trade causes.

## MEV and transaction ordering

On-chain execution shares a resource — a block's ordering — creating costs with no equity counterpart. Kulkarni, Diamandis and Chitra (2022/2023) formalise two games. Routing MEV: searchers' insertion and reordering shift both optimal and selfish routing, and if a sandwich attack's impact is localised in a suitable sense the price of anarchy is constant, independent of network size. Reordering MEV: for $n$ user trades the maximum price impact caused by sandwiching relative to the average is $O(\log n)$ — bounded but real, growing with the number of trades in a block. Canidio and Fritsch (2023/2025) propose the design response: a function-maximizing AMM that batches trades before execution at a single clearing price, which under arbitrageur competition eliminates arbitrage profits (LVR) and sandwich attacks. Simulating with Binance data for 11 pairs, they find a lower bound on liquidity provision that is, for the most part, slightly higher than the historical Uniswap v3 return; they assume the batched AMM receives 30% of Uniswap v3 volume as noise trading and note that in one study pool about 60% of volume is noise trading. For execution, MEV protection is a bounded but real cost, and the route — public mempool, private channel, batched venue — is priced alongside gas and fees.

## A claim that could not be verified

A widely repeated statement holds that optimising an execution against the *complete fee schedule* yields "over 60% potential savings". This chapter could not verify it. Every obtained source was converted to text and searched for savings and cost-reduction phrasings; no passage reports a saving above 60% from fee-schedule optimisation. The adjacent 60% figures mean something else: in He et al. the 52%–90% (or 60%–90%) is the annualised deviation of the perpetual from its no-arbitrage benchmark, in Canidio and Fritsch 60% is the share of volume that is noise trading, in Barbon and Ranaldo 60% is the DEX volume their sample covers, and in Wu et al. 60% and 89% are the shares of volume two searchers direct to one builder. The papers that do model a full fee schedule — He et al.'s tiers and negotiated zero-fee market-maker contracts, the Binance schedule itself, Lehar et al.'s 1/5/30/100 basis-point tiers — report Sharpe ratios, no-arbitrage bands and liquidity shares, not a percentage saving. The nearest *measured* execution-saving number is far below 60%: Xi and Moallemi's 2.02 basis points of routing sub-optimality, of which the within-set-switching part is only 0.05467 basis points. Diamandis et al.'s "over 50%" is a solver comparison on test instances, not a cost saving, and it is not 60%. The figure should be treated as unsourced until a specific work is named; this chapter does not repeat it as fact.

A related gap: Aleti and Mizrach (2021) could not be obtained in full text. Their publisher abstract reports a BTC spot–futures market of roughly USD 146–160 million per day, median spot trade sizes under USD 1,300 against over USD 18,000 on the CME, average spreads of 0.0298% (about 2.98 basis points), and trades over USD 1 million moving the market by less than one basis point. These are abstract-level facts, not verified against the full text.

## What this means for a crypto executor

For a **small, uninformed** trader the binding cost is the proportional fee plus the fixed gas charge. Trading on a CEX, and posting rather than taking where the schedule allows, is the literature-supported default, because gas dominates DEX cost at small notional (Barbon and Ranaldo); targeting a higher fee tier by concentrating volume, and paying fees in the venue's token where discounted, follows from the schedule being tiered by 30-day volume.

For a **large** trader the cost is dominated by depth and impact. The square-root law applies with the same exponent and a coefficient close to that of equities (Donier and Bonart), so the schedule is calibrated with the Chapter 5 machinery — but the venue choice shifts, because DEXs become cost-competitive above roughly USD 10,000 and can be split across fee tiers (Lehar et al.). On-chain routing should be gas-aware and use execution-time pool state, since a one-block lag costs of the order of 1.3 to 1.8 basis points (Xi and Moallemi), and should use MEV protection where flow is exposed to sandwiching.

For a **cross-venue or basis** trader the binding constraints are settlement latency and funding timing. Cross-exchange deviations are large and persistent because capital is stuck, not because costs are high (Makarov and Schoar; Hautsch et al.), so the design question is how to hold inventory, and at what default risk, to convert a price gap into profit. Perpetual basis trades must be evaluated against the no-arbitrage band implied by the *actual* fee tier and the funding rule (He et al.; Ackerer et al.), with the horizon set so expected funding benefit exceeds the round-trip fee (Donnelly et al.).

Two of these conclusions are this chapter's judgement rather than a specific literature result. That the fee tier and the funding rule jointly define the appropriate horizon is an inference from the separate results (He et al.'s band, Donnelly et al.'s funding-driven schedule); no paper in the corpus poses that joint optimisation and solves it. And prioritising execution-time pool state over exotic splitting on-chain is a reading of Xi and Moallemi's decomposition — most of their dollar shortfall is pool activation, most of the timing loss is staleness — not a claim those authors make about strategy design.

## Key points

- Crypto execution differs from equity execution in six structural ways: 24/7 trading with no halt, venue fragmentation without a consolidated tape, tiered maker–taker fees, per-eight-hour funding on perpetuals, withdrawal fees plus settlement latency, and the coexistence of centralised books with AMMs.
- Binance's spot schedule is tiered by 30-day volume from 0.1000% maker/taker at retail to 0.0110%/0.0230% at VIP 9, with maker below taker at every tier and zero negotiable for market makers, so the maker–taker spread and volume tiering — not a single proportional fee — are what an execution model must ingest.
- Cross-exchange deviations are large and persistent — up to USD 2 billion of potential arbitrage profit over December 2017–February 2018 and a US–Korea premium reaching 40% — because capital is stuck and settlement is slow, while spreads (1–10 basis points) and fees (0.1%–0.25%) are far smaller.
- The crypto price-impact law is the *same* square-root law as equities: $\langle I(Q)\rangle\propto Q^{0.5}$ with a normalised Y-ratio of about 0.9, holding over four decades on a venue with almost no HFT, so no crypto-specific exponent is needed.
- The AMM execution cost is the curvature of the trading function, unit cost linear in trade size and inversely proportional to pool depth, structurally different from a book's discrete-level liquidity, and predictable loss/LVR is the floor beneath the taker's cost.
- The nearest measured routing-saving number is small — 2.02 basis points on average, USD 24 million over USD 120 billion, with the within-set-switching part only 0.05467 basis points — so MEV protection and timely pool state matter more than exotic splitting.
- The funding rate is a clamped function of the futures–spot premium ($\kappa=1$, $\gamma=0.05\%$, $\iota=0.01\%$ on Binance) that makes the perpetual's effective maturity random, so it is a cost the execution horizon must account for rather than a free signal.
- The claim that "optimising against the full fee schedule yields over 60% potential savings" could not be verified in any obtained source; the adjacent 60% figures are an annualised basis deviation, a noise-trading share and sample coverage, and the closest measured saving is expressed in basis points.
