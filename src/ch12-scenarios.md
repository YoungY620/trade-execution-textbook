# Scenarios side by side: institutions and retail, high and low frequency, informed and uninformed

The chapters to this point each solved one version of the same problem. [Chapter 3](./ch03-microstructure.md) said where execution cost comes from, [Chapter 4](./ch04-implementation-shortfall.md) made it measurable, [Chapter 5](./ch05-almgren-chriss.md) and [Chapter 6](./ch06-beyond-ac.md) optimised a schedule, [Chapter 7](./ch07-market-making.md) put a dealer on the other side, [Chapter 8](./ch08-order-placement.md) went inside the order, and [Chapters 10](./ch10-learning.md) and [11](./ch11-crypto.md) handled the cases the closed form misses. This chapter adds no model. It puts three splits side by side — institution against retail, high frequency against lower frequency, informed against uninformed — and asks, for each cell, what actually changes.

The answer is not the mathematics: the Almgren–Chriss objective, the Kyle slope and the adverse-selection spread are the same objects for everyone. What changes is which term of the cost ledger binds, and therefore what the control variable is. Written down, the optimal execution of a large informed fund and of a retail order are not one policy at different parameters but two different problems.

## Institutions and retail: impact and leakage versus spread and fees

An institutional order is large relative to the volume it must consume. In Chapter 5's calibrated case a block of one million shares is 20 percent of a five-million-share day. At that size the dominant terms are the ones Chapter 3's ledger assigns to the aggressor: market impact, which every model from Chapter 5 onward exists to control, and the informational part of it, which does not come back. The order is also a multi-period object — Bertsimas and Lo, citing Chan and Lakonishok, report that only 20 percent of the market value of institutional packages completes within a day and over half is spread over four or more trading days. A five-million-share day cannot be bought at one price; it is a metaorder whose impact Chapter 13 calibrates with the square-root law and whose slices are scheduled, split and placed by the machinery of Chapters 5, 6 and 8.

The binding second problem is leakage. An order worked over days can have its direction and size read, and Chapter 8's predatory-trading result says what that invites: a predator front-runs a visible liquidation need, the price overshoots, and the distressed trader's excess cost is the predator's profit. The institutional optimum is therefore a schedule shaped by the half-life of Chapter 5, concealed by the order-type and display choices of Chapter 8 — iceberg and hidden orders, dark venues as substitutes — and, where Chapter 3's Kyle slope can be escaped at all, routed away from the venues where its flow is most observable.

A retail order is small enough that the impact term is negligible by construction. The same ledger that charges the institution for impact charges the retailer for the spread and the explicit fee, and the two do not scale together. Almgren and Chriss estimate the fixed temporary cost as half the bid–ask spread plus fees; for a large order that is the smallest term, and for a small one it is essentially the whole cost. Chapter 4 makes the empirical case: in Leinweber's 13,651-trade sample the largest trades cost far less than the block-cost models predicted, the highest and lowest percentage costs sat on the smallest trades, and small trades dominated aggregate cost. Mittal's illustration gives the scale — a 30,000-share order in a stock trading 30 million shares a day is better done in minutes, to save opportunity cost, than worked against VWAP for a day. The retail optimum is near the opposite corner from the institutional one: splitting a small order adds fees and forfeits the immediacy it wants, so the problem is order type and venue, not a risk-aversion parameter.

What separates the two is not only size but the nature of the cost. The institutional cost is an equilibrium object; the retail cost is largely a posted one. In Kyle's model the slope of price on order flow is set by the ratio of noise trading to unresolved private information, so the retail order is, in Kyle's terms, largely the noise that supplies the depth the informed order consumes. Chapter 8's hidden-order results make the same point from the other side: opacity that conceals a large patient order can widen the inside spread, a benefit to the institutional trader and a cost to the retail one. The institution buys a reduction in its own impact; the retailer pays part of it in the spread. The venue-side arrangements that decide how much of the retail cost is returned as price improvement are treated under regulation below.

## High frequency and lower frequency: order-book time versus schedule time

The second split is time scale, and it decides which chapter's mathematics is the right one.

At high frequency the decision is the order-book event: a quote, a fill, a cancellation. The control is not a rate over a horizon but a pair of quote distances and a choice between posting and taking. That is Chapter 7 exactly. The state is inventory, cash and the mid; Avellaneda–Stoikov's reservation price $r(s,q,t)=s-q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)$ shifts both quotes against inventory; their optimal spread, $\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)+\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln(1+\gamma/k)$, splits into a risk term and a liquidity term; and once the same agent may send market orders as well as post, the problem becomes a quasi-variational inequality in which limit orders are a continuous control and market orders an impulse. Chapter 8 supplies the micro-statistics that decide the fill: queue position is worth a measurable fraction of a spread, and the front of the queue on a large-tick stock was worth about 0.21 to 0.26 ticks more than an average position.

The mathematical consequence of a short horizon is that the risk term dominates and the schedule term disappears. In Chapter 5 the trade's half-life is $\theta=1/\kappa$ with $\kappa \sim \sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}$, and the trajectory degenerates toward immediate liquidation as $\kappa T \to \infty$. A high-frequency taker is in that limit: it crosses because waiting costs more than the spread, and its residual risk is intraday inventory, not an overnight position. The high-frequency maker takes the other side and is paid the spread for standing there, which is why one adverse-selection term is a cost to the taker and a surcharge in the maker's quote.

At lower and medium frequency the decision is the schedule. Chapter 5's objective and Chapter 6's corrections apply, and the horizon is long enough that risk aversion and the impact coefficient both matter rather than one dominating. This is also where the measurement layer lives: Chapter 4's transaction cost analysis and Chapter 13's capture–attribute–re-estimate loop exist because a schedule's parameters are estimated from data, carry a vintage, and drift with regime. A high-frequency desk does not run a quarterly refit; a medium-frequency executor cannot avoid one.

Read together, the same algebra strengthens or degenerates. The hyperbolic-sine path is a smooth curve between two corners; push the horizon short and it becomes the immediate-liquidation corner, push it long and it becomes the equal-split line. Chapter 6's closed-loop rules are the intermediate case, and they are instructive about where adaptivity pays: Schied and Schöneborn's feedback strategy collapses to the deterministic exponential path under constant absolute risk aversion, so reacting to the market buys nothing unless the state carries a signal.

## Informed and uninformed: half-life versus pure risk

The third split is the one this book cares most about, because every chapter before this one was written for an executor holding information about value.

To an uninformed trader, its own permanent impact is a cost: it pays to move the price toward a level it does not know is right. To an informed trader the same permanent impact is not a cost at all. Chapter 3 states the asymmetry directly — the permanent part of impact is the market discovering what the order meant, and the informed trader realises it on the inventory the trade accumulates. The same Kyle slope that is a fee to the uninformed is a return to the informed. That one observation reverses the problem. An uninformed trader minimises impact; an informed trader trades fast enough that the information is realised before it decays, and slow enough that temporary impact and leakage do not eat the gain.

The variable that arbitrates is the information half-life. Chapters 6 and 7 give the signal-driven answer. Lehalle and Neuman's optimal execution with a Markovian signal is linear in both the initial inventory and the signal, and need not be monotone: a signal can make a liquidating agent buy. Cartea and Wang's market maker does the same, pushing away the risk-side quote as the signal grows and, past a threshold, sending market orders in the direction of the signal. Chapter 5's half-life $\theta$ is the deterministic shadow of that logic: it is the time in which the position decays, and a live signal shortens the effective decay below what a pure risk frame would choose.

The uninformed case is not a degenerate special case; it is a different problem with its own answer. With no view, the cost that remains is the level and the variance of the price at which the trade completes — Chapter 5's $E(x)+\lambda V(x)$ with no drift, the pure-risk frame. Chapter 4's target-close and VWAP results make that operational: a cash-flow or index-following order wants to track a benchmark, not to escape a moving value, so its optimal curve is the relative volume curve, or a schedule that stiffens toward the close. An uninformed executor may even turn around and quote, earning the spread Chapter 7 assigns to the market maker, because the adverse-selection surcharge it would face is small exactly when its own flow is uninformative. That last step — that the same agent may switch sides once it has no information — is this chapter's judgement, resting on the sign reversal in Chapter 7.

The six rows below cross the three splits where they genuinely change the answer. Each cell names the objective, the control, the binding constraint, the shape of the optimal policy, the dominant cost and the benchmark; the last column records where in the book it comes from. A cell marked "not covered here" is a term no written chapter develops.

Two contrasts stand out. The objective is the same functional in four of the six rows — expected cost plus a risk penalty — but the binding term differs, so the control differs: the institution's control is a schedule and a concealment plan, the retailer's an order type. And the informed/uninformed split cuts across the other two rather than lying alongside them, since a high-frequency maker and a large informed fund both trade fast while one is paid the spread and the other captures a move.

## When X moves: three comparative statics

Three parameters decide which way any of these policies moves, and each is a Chapter 5 or Chapter 6 parameter read through the split.

**A shorter information half-life.** When the information half-life shortens, the optimal policy moves from the moderate, impact-driven pace toward the aggressive end, because the trade's intrinsic time $\theta=1/\kappa$, with $\kappa \sim \sqrt{\lambda\sigma^2/\eta}$, is what sets the pace, and a shrinking window over which the value is expected to persist is equivalent to a rise in the effective $\kappa$; the signal-driven solutions of Chapter 6 say the same thing directly, since the optimal rate is monotone in the expected signal, and Chapter 7's maker converts a large enough signal into market orders. The cost of waiting is that the information is gone; the cost of hurrying is leakage and impact.

**Thinner depth.** When market depth falls — so that $1/\lambda$ in Chapter 3's sense is smaller and the temporary-impact coefficient $\eta$ is larger — the optimal policy moves from the faster hyperbolic-sine path toward the slower, more spread-out end, because a higher $\eta$ lowers $\kappa$ and lengthens $\theta$. Under the transient-impact picture of Chapter 6 the shape changes as well as the pace: the optimum moves from a purely continuous flow toward boundary lumps plus a constant interior rate, set by the book's resilience $\rho$ rather than by the static impact parameters.

**Higher risk aversion.** When risk aversion rises, the optimal policy moves toward the front-loaded end, because $\kappa$ rises with $\lambda$ and the half-life shortens; Chapter 6 adds a qualitative step, since Schied and Schöneborn tie the direction of adaptation to the shape of the utility function, with increasing absolute risk aversion giving aggressive-in-the-money behaviour and constant absolute risk aversion giving the deterministic path. Higher risk aversion is thus not just a faster version of the same schedule; past a point it changes whether the schedule should react to the price at all.

## Constraints the scenario does not capture

Three constraints sit outside the six cells and change the answer for everyone in them.

### Best execution

Execution is a regulated service, and the regulation imposes obligations the models do not choose. In the United States the Order Protection Rule requires every trading centre to prevent executions at prices inferior to protected quotations elsewhere — those that are immediately and automatically accessible — the Access Rule caps the per-share fee for removing liquidity, and the Sub-Penny Rule fixes a one-cent minimum increment. These rules define the feasible set a router must respect, and they also decide which benchmark a broker may fairly report against: because the arrival price is known at the decision instant while VWAP and the close are functions of the price path, a broker working to a random benchmark and a client holding it to the arrival price are measuring different things. Chapter 13 develops the governance of that choice; best execution is the legal reason it cannot be left implicit. The reading of best execution as a constraint on benchmark choice rather than on the schedule is this chapter's judgement.

### Non-view flow: index changes and cash

Not every order carries a view. Index rebalancing, a cash contribution or a redemption forces a trade whose direction is fixed by something other than a forecast. Chapter 3's ledger treats this as the uninformed case, and Chapter 5 gives it a clean answer: with no drift, the objective is the pure-risk frame $E(x)+\lambda V(x)$, and the schedule is chosen for cost and variance alone. Chapter 4's benchmark machinery then applies directly — a cash-flow order that must be complete by a date is a target-close or VWAP problem, not an information problem, and its optimal curve follows the relative volume curve or stiffens toward the close. The failure mode when this is done badly is the Flash Crash, where a large sell program executed at a fixed fraction of volume "without regard to price or time" and met a book that withdrew rather than absorbed. That such flow may also be run as a market maker — quoting and earning the spread, on the strength of Chapter 7's small adverse-selection surcharge for uninformative flow — is this chapter's judgement.

### Principal and agent

Execution cost is produced by one party and borne by another. The benchmark decides who bears it: Chapter 13 records Hasbrouck's example in which a broker attributes the trend component of a three-day order to the benchmark and leaves the residual unexplained, and the choice of VWAP over arrival changes which agent is credited with the move. The fee structure does the same at the venue level. A maker-taker schedule pays a rebate to the resting order and charges the taker, so the split of the fee changes the broker's routing incentive; the empirical record is that raising the taker fee and the rebate together can narrow the quoted spread while raising the investor's total cost, and that payment for order flow pays the broker for routing retail flow while the customer receives price improvement that, in equities, is real and most valuable when the spread is at its one-cent minimum — around 20 cents per 100 shares in equity payment for order flow against 40 cents in options. Treating benchmark attribution and fee design as one principal–agent problem, rather than as separate measurement and market-structure questions, is this chapter's judgement; the mechanism rests on the attribution argument above and on the venue-fee evidence.

## Key points

- The three splits — institution against retail, high against lower frequency, informed against uninformed — do not partition traders; they partition cost structures, and what changes across a split is which term of the cost ledger binds and therefore what the control variable is.
- An institutional order is large relative to ADV and worked over days, so its costs are impact, opportunity cost and leakage and its optimum is a schedule plus a concealment plan; a retail order is small enough that impact is negligible, so its costs are the spread and the fee and its optimum is an order type and a venue.
- The frequency split decides which mathematics applies: at high frequency the control is the quote distance and the post-versus-take choice, and the half-life limit degenerates to immediate liquidation, while at medium frequency the schedule, the risk-aversion parameter and the TCA calibration loop all matter.
- The informed split reverses the objective: the permanent part of impact is the realisation of value to an informed trader and a cost to an uninformed one, so the informed trade is paced by the information half-life while the uninformed trade faces only the pure-risk frame $E(x)+\lambda V(x)$.
- The six-row decision table gives each scenario its objective, control, binding constraint, policy shape, dominant cost and benchmark; the objective is the same functional in four rows, but the binding term and the control differ.
- Three comparative statics move every policy: a shorter information half-life pushes toward the aggressive end, thinner depth pushes toward the slower and more spread-out end, and higher risk aversion front-loads the schedule and can change whether it reacts to the price at all.
- Best execution, non-view flow and the principal–agent relation sit outside the six cells: regulation fixes the feasible set and the fair benchmark, index and cash-flow trades are the uninformed case the pure-risk frame answers, and benchmark and fee choices decide which party bears the cost.
