# Reinforcement learning and adaptive execution

Chapter 5 solved liquidation in closed form and Chapter 6 turned that solution into a feedback rule: aggressive-in-the-money, utility-based and volume-tracked strategies that react to price. Every one of those results needs the same three things known in advance — the impact functions $g$ and $h$, the volatility $\sigma$, and an objective quadratic in inventory or cost. Hambly, Xu and Yang state the residual objection plainly: the Almgren–Chriss optimum is "a pre-planned strategy that does not depend on real-time market conditions", and "the mis-specification of the dynamics and market impacts may lead to undesirable strategies and potential losses". When impact is concave, when the liquidity coefficient is latent and time-varying, when the objective is not mean–variance, or when the decision includes *where* to post as well as *how much* to trade, the closed form is not available. Reinforcement learning (RL) is the alternative: treat execution as a control problem whose model is unknown and learn the policy from data, aiming at the same target — minimise implementation shortfall (IS) or a cost-plus-risk functional.

## Why learning, and what "the same target" means

The classical route computes the value function analytically from a model; the learning route estimates it from transitions and never sees the model. The two are not mathematical rivals — they address the same objective, and RL is used precisely where the first has no answer. Three families of problem motivate it. The **model is misspecified**: impact is empirically concave, and the temporary-impact coefficient moves within a day and is unobservable; Chapter 6 showed that analytical corrections need extra structure, whereas RL can infer the coefficient from prices. The **state is high-dimensional and non-Markovian**: a real decision uses the book, recent order flow, one's own fills and the remaining schedule, which a tabular method or a PDE cannot carry but a network can. The **action space is joint**: the AC problem chooses a schedule, while a broker chooses a schedule *and* an order type *and* a book level, and only the learned formulation has so far controlled all three in one policy.

One structural assumption makes execution tractable for RL: the state splits into **private variables** (elapsed time, remaining inventory) and **market variables**, and during training the agent's actions are assumed not to move the market variables.

## The formal object

An execution problem becomes an MDP $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\gamma)$ once the state, action, transitions and reward are chosen. The horizon is finite, and every episode ends the same way: inventory still held at $T$ is force-liquidated. The table records how the works this chapter draws on define those objects.

| Work | State | Action | Reward | Algorithm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nevmyvaka et al. 2006 | $t$, $i$, plus $R$ market variables (spread, imbalance, immediate market-order cost, signed 15 s volume) | price to re-quote *all* remaining inventory, relative to the ask/bid | cash proceeds of partial fills; terminal market order | Q-learning with backward induction |
| Hendricks–Wilcox 2014 | $t,i$, percentile spread and volume states, conditioning hour | scale of the AC child order, $\beta\in[0,2]$ in steps of 0.25 | realised IS of the child order walked through the book | tabular Q-learning |
| Ning et al. 2018 | time, inventory, optional mid-price and quadratic variation | shares to sell this period, $x_t\in[0,q_t]$ | mark-to-market cash minus $a\,(x_{T_k}/M_k)^2$ | double DQN |
| Lin–Beling 2020 | raw level-2 (top-5 prices, volumes), inventory, elapsed time | shares traded, in $[0,2\cdot\mathrm{TWAP}]$ | sparse terminal signal vs TWAP | PPO |
| Karpe et al. 2020 | normalised time, inventory, spread, best-level imbalance, one-period and since-start returns | (execution size) × (market order, or limit at top 1/2/3 levels) | slippage × executed fraction | double DQN |
| Macrì–Lillo 2024 | inventory, time, optionally mid-price | shares sold per step | IS | double DQN |
| Fang et al. 2021 | minute price/volume and book; teacher also sees the future | executed quantity per minute | normalised instantaneous reward | RL teacher, distilled student |
| FlowOE 2025 | Heston-environment state | action distribution over trades | imitation of an expert ensemble | flow-matching policy |

Reward design is where the papers disagree most. Nevmyvaka et al. and Hendricks–Wilcox use IS directly. Ning et al. penalise the square of shares per second as a proxy for walking the book. Lin and Beling reject IS as "noisy and nonstationary … a surrogate reward function, but not a direct optimization goal", and reward relative performance against TWAP instead: a sparse terminal $+1$ when the policy's IS beats TWAP by at least ten percent, $-1$ when it falls short, and $0$ in between and at intermediate steps.

## The first large-scale solution: Nevmyvaka, Feng and Kearns (2006)

This is the founding application. Over a fixed horizon $H$ the agent sells $V$ shares in a simulator driven by real INET (NASDAQ) order flow, with its own limit orders inserted into reconstructed books. The state is $\langle t,i,o_1,\dots,o_R\rangle$: elapsed-time and inventory coordinates at resolutions $T$ and $I$, plus market variables summarising spread, bid–ask volume imbalance, immediate market-order cost, and signed transaction volume over the last fifteen seconds. The action is a single limit price at which to reposition *all* remaining inventory, relative to the ask; $a=0$ joins the touch and $a>0$ crosses the spread. The reward is the cash realised by partial fills, with any residual force-liquidated at the end, and cost is measured in basis points against the mid at the start of the episode.

The algorithm is Q-learning with backward induction, made cheap by the private/public independence assumption: because the agent's actions are taken not to move market variables during training, the optimiser visits every $\{t,i\}$ cell against every observed book, at a run time independent of $R$. The test-set simulations do include the policy's own impact, which is how the assumption is validated.

On one and a half years of millisecond INET data (twelve months train, six test; AMZN, QCOM, NVDA; $V\in\{5{,}000,10{,}000\}$ shares; $H\in\{2,8\}$ minutes), performance against optimised submit-and-leave improves by **27.16% to 35.50%** using private variables only, with either $T$ or $I$ raised to 8. Adding market variables lowers cost further: spread alone **+7.97%**, spread plus immediate market-order cost **+8.69%**, and spread plus immediate cost plus signed volume **+12.85%**; volume imbalance on its own adds only **+0.13%**, because it predicts cost without changing the cost-minimising action. Overall the authors report improvement "of 50% or more over S&L policies and several times over market orders". The policy becomes more aggressive as time runs out and inventory remains. Two limits are the authors' own: other traders do not react strategically, and fees and latency are ignored. Structurally the action fixes quantity at all remaining inventory, so this is placement without scheduling.

## Learning a correction to Almgren–Chriss: Hendricks and Wilcox (2014)

Hendricks and Wilcox keep the AC closed form as a *prior* and learn only a deviation from it. The agent does not choose a schedule; it chooses the proportion of the AC child order to trade each period, $a_{jt}=\beta_j\,\mathrm{AC}_t$ with $\beta_j\in[0,2]$ in steps of 0.25, and residual volume is executed at the end. The state is $\langle t_n,i_n,s_n,v_n\rangle$ — elapsed time, remaining inventory, and percentile-bucketed spread and bid/ask-volume states — with the trading hour conditioning which distributions define those buckets. The reward is the realised IS of the child order as it walks the book, and the method is tabular Q-learning with the finite-horizon MDP made convergent by an absorbing state ($\gamma=1$).

On five-level depth for the 166 South African ALSI stocks over 2012, with six-month training, the RL model lowers the median IS of AC by **up to 10.3%** for short horizons ($T=4$, i.e. 20 minutes) and by **4.8%** on average. Per-cell results run from **15 basis points for SBK at 16:00–17:00** to **−7 basis points for SAB**, and finer state grids raise the share of correct actions without improving IS. The important caveat is the authors' own: because the reward is risk-neutral IS, the RL trajectories carry a *higher* standard deviation of IS than AC (0.17–0.26 against 0.13–0.14), so the gain is bought with extra execution risk — a defect to repair by putting variance into the reward.

## Deep reinforcement learning

**Double deep Q-learning.** Ning, Lin and Jaimungal scale the tabular method with a network, market orders only. The essential state is elapsed time and remaining inventory, with transformed mid-price and the previous period's quadratic variation as optional additions; because the admissible action set changes with inventory, the network takes $(s,x)$ and returns $Q(s,x)$. The reward is mark-to-market cash minus a quadratic penalty $a\,(x_{T_k}/M_k)^2$ on shares per second. The implementation is Double DQN with experience replay, $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration, a six-layer twenty-node ReLU network and RMSprop, with $a=0.01$, replay of $N=10{,}000$, target update every $\rho=15$ iterations, $\gamma=0.99$, and pre-training on the boundary cases of holding-then-dumping and dumping immediately. On nine instruments over 2 January 2017 – 30 March 2018, hours 11–13, $Q_0=20$ lots (2,000 shares), one hour split into five decision periods, the median P&L gain over TWAP is **+11.63 basis points for INTC** and **+11.96** with quadratic variation, **+5.97/+5.89 for MSFT**, **+10.13 for NTAP**, **+14.68/+15.72 for VOD**, **+2.92 for AAPL** and **+2.61 for FB**; $P(\Delta>0)$ is 95.8–97.8% for INTC, MSFT and NTAP but only 77.4% for AAPL, and **GOOG and AMZN show no significant edge** (AMZN mean −0.28). Learned policies sell faster when the price is high, when little time remains, and when quadratic variation is high.

**End-to-end policy gradients.** Lin and Beling replace the hand-designed state and shaped reward with raw level-2 data (top five bid/ask prices and volumes) plus remaining inventory and elapsed time, and a sparse benchmark-relative reward. Actions are share counts in $[0,2\cdot\mathrm{TWAP}]$, sized so TWAP would consume at least the second-best price level on average. The algorithm is PPO-clip in two architectures — a two-layer 128-unit network feeding an LSTM of cell size 128 that emits policy and value, or a network with recent book levels stacked as inputs — with zero ending inventory enforced by combining the last two steps. Trained on WRDS millisecond TAQ for 2018 across fourteen US equities (one-minute horizon, five-second decisions; January–September train, October–December test), the agents converge in **fewer than 200,000 steps** and beat TWAP, VWAP, AC and two DQN variants on most names. On Boeing the mean $\Delta\mathrm{IS}$ against TWAP is 72.53 for PPO-LSTM, against 91.35 for DQN (Ning), −6.82 for sparse-reward DQN and −0.78 for AC. The authors observe that Ning's method oscillates dangerously on some stocks.

**Multi-agent simulation.** Karpe, Fang, Ma and Wang train a double-DQN agent inside ABIDES, an agent-based LOB simulator with realistic latency that replays historical NASDAQ order flow. It is, by the authors' claim, "the first formulation with optimal execution and optimal placement combined in the action space": the action multiplies the TWAP child size by a scalar $a\in\{0.1,0.5,1.0,\dots,2.5\}$ and then chooses a placement — market order, 100% at the top level, 50/50 across the top two, or one third on each of the top three. The reward is slippage times the executed fraction. On five NASDAQ stocks (CSCO, IBM, INTC, MSFT, YHOO) from 13 January to 6 February 2003, 30-second periods over 10:00–15:30, trained on nine days and tested on the following nine, the agent **converges to TWAP after about nine days of training**, placing top-level limit or market orders. The authors attribute the collapse to the 30-second cadence, which cannot see shorter-horizon signals — a warning that a rich action space is not sufficient without a signal at the right timescale.

## Learning liquidity the model does not know: Macrì and Lillo (2024)

Macrì and Lillo test RL against AC where AC is wrong by construction: the temporary and permanent impact coefficients are time-varying and *latent*. When impact rises deterministically and the state is only inventory and time, the learned IS is 0.2401 against the theoretical 0.1449 and TWAP's 0.2326 — no better than TWAP, at −4.76 basis points relative to theory. Adding the mid-price drops the learned IS to 0.1944, only −2.42 basis points from theory. The decisive experiment is *mixed training*: train on both increasing- and decreasing-impact trajectories (10,000 each) and test on one, which is what production requires. Here TWAP is the natural agnostic benchmark: inventory-plus-time gives $\Delta$P&L against TWAP of −0.92 basis points, while inventory-plus-time-plus-price attains a learned IS of 0.1319 and **+5.2 basis points against TWAP** — beating the agnostic benchmark and even the theoretical optimum's average cost, having inferred the impact direction and magnitude from price alone. A purely model-free method has recovered what the analytics had to be told. The study sells only and omits the spread.

## Imitation and offline learning

Two features make execution an awkward fit for online RL: exploring in a real market costs money and is not repeatable, and a policy trained by trial and error meets only the states its own errors create, which biases it away from the market. Hence the pull toward learning from demonstrations or a fixed dataset.

Fang and co-authors train a **teacher** that observes perfect future information and a **student** that sees only the current observable state, and transfer the teacher's action distribution to the student by policy distillation. The student learns the oracle's behaviour without future-information leakage at deployment, and — unusually — a *single* policy is trained across instruments. On China A-shares (January 2017 – June 2019, evaluated on the CSI 800), the distilled policy reaches a Price Advantage of **6.17 basis points** against TWAP's 0 (AC 2.33, VWAP 0.32, DDQN 4.13, PPO 2.52), with a gain–loss ratio of 1.35; a case study finds DDQN trades almost identically day after day while the distilled policy follows the price.

FlowOE goes further: it learns a generative flow-matching model of the action distribution conditioned on the observable state, imitating an *ensemble* of experts — TWAP, VWAP, an Almgren–Chriss approximation adjusted for non-linear impact, a Heston-optimal expert and a PPO expert. The environment is Heston stochastic volatility with square-root concave impact. Out of sample over 10,000 trials with $\beta=0.5$, in the high-volatility high-impact case the learned "Shortcut" policy trained on the PPO experts achieves an IS of **189,332** and an overall cost of **269,147**, against TWAP 211,139 / 476,592, VWAP 210,908 / 455,611, the AC approximation 210,880 / 427,384 and the Heston-optimal expert 210,592 / 406,607 — beating both the expert it imitates and every classical baseline on IS, risk and cost. Imitation thus sidesteps reward design by letting an expert's actions encode the objective, though everything here is simulated.

## A shared formalism: execution and hedging

Kolm and Ritter's work on dynamic replication and hedging uses RL for the option-side analogue of the same problem, and it belongs here for a formal reason. The original article (2019) sits behind a paywall and was not obtained; the method is described only at the level the authors' public slides and a companion survey support. Its shape is the execution MDP's: the state is portfolio and market, the action is a hedge trade, the reward is terminal replication error penalised by transaction cost, and the aim is to let a learned policy internalise costs that the Black–Scholes delta plus a fixed no-trade band pays for. Execution and hedging are the same control problem with different objectives, so a reward-normalisation or policy-class trick developed for one transfers to the other. Any quantitative claim about Kolm and Ritter's own results is unverified here.

## Inductive bias and generalisation across assets

A policy trained on one stock should ideally trade another. The theoretical lever is **microstructure invariance** (Kyle and Obizhaeva, 2016): measured in *business time* — the arrival rate of risk transfers — the distribution of bet sizes and the expected cost of a bet are the same across assets. Writing $W$ for dollars of risk transferred per day, the arrival rate of bets scales as the two-thirds power and the average bet as the one-third power, $\gamma_{jt}\propto W_{jt}^{2/3}$ and $\bar Q\propto W_{jt}^{1/3}$, while the percentage spread is proportional to the cube root of (return variance / dollar volume). These are units in which size, time and price can be re-expressed so that one policy fits many assets; they were estimated on 400,000+ portfolio-transition orders over 2001–2005.

Tran and Yu test whether that buys anything. Their decision is minimal: rest a limit order at the best price and wait, or cross the spread. Policies are trained once on 954 stocks in 2019 and evaluated unchanged on 1,964 unseen stocks in 2020–21, 2,629 in 2025–26, on crypto pairs and on a second exchange. A policy that sees a stock only through its recent trade rate, trade size, spread and tick costs **within 0.015 spreads per share** of a policy whose training data included that stock — about a fifth of what learning saves over posting and waiting — and the stock's own history adds nothing. But invariant units beat raw units, z-scores and asset descriptors by **under one basis point**, most of the edge being a cost scale and information a learner can supply anyway: given those window statistics and the cost in spreads, raw features recover **81%** of the gap. **No representation tested extrapolates to a spread regime it never saw.** The authors also warn that a near-zero transfer gap to a pooled comparator is weak evidence of a good representation, since the comparator registers a representation's loss only in proportion to the test asset's share of its data.

## An honest assessment

Several failure modes recur and are acknowledged by the literature. The simulation-to-market gap is the largest: most studies replay historical data or run an agent-based simulator, and only a few (Tran and Yu on exact order-by-order Nasdaq replay; Karpe et al.'s ABIDES with reactive agents) let the agent's own orders move the book. Where they do not, "the market is resilient" is an *assumption* — in Lin and Beling's words the "core assumption of this article and also all previous research applying RL for optimal trade execution", because training on historical data "cannot account for the permanent market impact." Reward design is the second: the same problem is solved with IS, a quadratic rate penalty, and a sparse TWAP-relative signal, and the papers argue openly about which is right; Hendricks and Wilcox show that a risk-neutral IS reward *raises* the variance of IS, the very criterion the classical solution was built to control. Sample efficiency is the third: the works here span 45,000 episodes, 200,000 steps, 8 million steps and 300,000 transitions, far more data than calibrating an AC model would take. Out-of-distribution generalisation is the fourth: invariant units transfer within the range seen in training but not to a new spread regime, and a policy trained on one liquidity direction must infer the other from price or fall back to TWAP.

This chapter judges, as its own assessment rather than the sources', that reported improvements must be read through three filters. They are highly sensitive to the baseline: the method that beats submit-and-leave by 50% beats TWAP by nothing in Macrì and Lillo's inventory-plus-time case, and beats it on only seven of nine names in Ning et al. They are sensitive to the cost model — almost every study here assumes no fees, no latency and resilient books, and fee structure could easily consume a single-digit-basis-point edge. And they are usually measured against a *theoretical* optimum computed under the same linear-impact assumptions the RL was invoked to escape, so "beats AC" can mean only that the agent reproduced the linear optimum the analytics already gave. Karpe et al.'s collapse to TWAP is the cleanest caution: a rich action space and a correct reward produce the classical answer when the state carries no signal at the decision cadence.

## Where this leaves Chapters 5 and 6

RL does not replace the stochastic control of Chapters 5 and 6, and none of its authors claims that. It approximates the *same* objective with data when the closed form is unavailable, and it inherits the classical solution as the yardstick (the AC efficient frontier, against which data-driven policies are plotted) and as the prior (Hendricks and Wilcox learn only a bounded correction to the AC trajectory, and Macrì and Lillo recover the optimum once the right state variable is present). The classical model still supplies the units — impact, risk aversion, the frontier — and the learned policy supplies the reaction to a market the model never saw. Read in that order, Chapters 5, 6 and this chapter are one story: solve it when you can, correct it when you know how it is wrong, and learn it when you cannot write it down.

## Key points

- The Almgren–Chriss optimum is a pre-planned schedule that ignores real-time conditions and depends on correctly specified impacts; RL is the data-driven alternative when impact is non-linear, liquidity is latent, the objective is not quadratic, or the action must include order placement.
- Execution is written as a finite-horizon MDP whose state splits into private variables (time, inventory) and market variables, whose action is a quantity and/or a price, and whose reward is realised cash, implementation shortfall, a rate penalty or a benchmark-relative signal.
- Nevmyvaka, Feng and Kearns' backward-induction Q-learning improves on optimised submit-and-leave by 27.16–35.50% with private variables, and by 12.85% more when spread, immediate cost and signed volume are added; volume imbalance alone adds only 0.13%.
- Hendricks and Wilcox learn only a bounded correction to the AC trajectory and cut median implementation shortfall by up to 10.3% for short horizons and 4.8% on average, but at a higher standard deviation of implementation shortfall — the price of a risk-neutral reward.
- Deep RL extends the state and the action: double DQN beats TWAP on seven of nine US equities (up to +11.96 basis points on INTC) but not on GOOG or AMZN, PPO on raw level-2 data converges in under 200,000 steps and beats TWAP, VWAP and AC on most of fourteen names, and a joint action space over size and placement nonetheless collapses to TWAP when the decision cadence hides the signal.
- Imitation and offline learning are attractive because exploring in a real market is costly and irreversible; oracle policy distillation and flow-matching imitation both beat classical baselines and can train a single policy across instruments.
- Microstructure invariance gives real but modest zero-shot transfer — within 0.015 spreads per share across unseen stocks and exchanges — and no representation tested extrapolates to an unseen spread regime.
- RL does not replace the stochastic control of Chapters 5 and 6: it approximates the same objective with data, inherits the AC frontier as its yardstick and, in the hybrid designs, the AC schedule as its prior.
